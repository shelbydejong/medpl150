Reflection on ChatGPT's affects on the Brain

I found myself unsurprised by the results shown in the article and what they said in the video. I think I can safely say that it would make sense to most people that writing an essay and thinking about it yourself will create a stronger sense of ownership, memory, and connection to the essay than if you wrote a short prompt and the essay was written for you. But I did enjoy the nuances of LLM use that the video got into, how you can productively use ChatGPT in a way that isn’t detrimental to your brain.
I thought the host of the video was a little bit grating as a person, but I understand that he was just playing devil’s advocate. This allowed the specialists to go into greater detail about what they were saying, and I liked his question about the effects of background noise and multitasking on the brain; it made me wonder about the neurological differences between living in a city versus in a more rural area. Is it even possible to be in a place without noise? How big of a concern should noise really be? After growing up in the suburbs and more rural areas of upstate New York, and now living in Queens, I find that there is noise everywhere, and you learn to tune out the different noises based on your environment. Although the city may have honking and the sounds of passing cars, there is the loud hum of cicadas upstate. But, I suppose one is more calming and probably has a less serious effect on the brain than the other.
I also found what the specialists said about depression, hope, and positivity really interesting. I had never heard the number they quoted that you should have 15% negativity and the rest be a positive outlook, and how continuous negativity is bad for your brain. It is so wild how you can change your whole life just through your outlook, through “rewiring” your brain. And the fact that it could be something so small, like how one of their final pieces of advice was to start your morning by saying “today is going to be a good day,” was really uplifting. The fact that multitasking, background noise, ADHD, and overwhelm can lead to a reduction in the parts of the brain that make us hopeful and positive is very striking when we live in a society where these things are omnipresent. As a species, we will all begin to experience less hope, and perhaps then have less motivation and less will to put in effort, which in turn sends us towards the use of AI. As the host said, humans are more likely to go for a quick hit of dopamine rather than the more difficult delayed gratification, and these quick dopamine hits are becoming easier and easier to access, meaning the choice to do the hard thing is even less tempting. For the best possible outcome for yourself and your brain, you have to keep choosing the more difficult option.
